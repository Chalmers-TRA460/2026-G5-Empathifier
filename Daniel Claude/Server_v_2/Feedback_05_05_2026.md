# Feedback 2 

It is great to see that you are working on the empathizer. David asked me to provide some feedback as well on your current approach, and forwarded the chat messages where you describe the solution with a small form for the clinician to fill out. You have done some great work so far. I will answer the points you wanted feedback on.

Does the form feel like the right amount of work for a busy clinician? Too much, too little?
Of course, I cannot speak for every clinician out there, but having talked to many colleagues, seen new software introduced in the clinic, and also worked a bit in the medtech business, I believe I have a pretty good feel for the "general opinion" of doctors. Perhaps surprisingly, they are typically strongly adverse to forms and structured input. The only time they like it is if it clearly saves loads of time, like if you would replace writing a long section of text with just one or two checkboxes. BUT, even then it is often causing more frustration than relief in the majority of cases, because reality is often more complex than what was anticipated when designing the checkboxes, so the case often does not conform to the checkboxes anyway. :P
We have another study where doctors are allowed to send in questions they would have liked to have answered by a chatbot (we are researching the information needs of clinicians from medical AI). Some doctors are already using commercial chatbots, and just sent in their real questions that they asked e.g. ChatGPT during their work. We expected long case reports, but may questions were more similar to:
"40 years man prostatitis treatment"
"liver tests after cholecystectomy"
"subsidies for caring for relative. summarize."
The above examples may give you a feel for what the doctor feels he/she has time to write.
So for the example you provided, I think it would be much more useful if the clinician could write (in a single field - no form), something along the lines of:
"4-6 weeks for full effect. liver damage very rare. Stop taking if abdominal pain, nausea or other symp. referral if no improvment." - This would be extremely useful, but may require the AI to see the patient's question to achieve correct coherent responses. What is the reason not to allow this? To avoid the AI making its own take on answering the question? Or to avoid patient data being sent to the LLM? The first is absolutely a true concern and would have to be solved by careful prompt engineering or even finetuning. The second is better solved by careful data security, I think, perhaps using local LLM:s so we don't have to send data to an external provider (though some digital scribes do send data externally, so that too can be done...).
Now, if this becomes too hard for the LLM, we can absolutely increase the expectation of the doctor to be writing at the level they would write without access to your solution:
"Hi! It takes 4-6 weeks for the medicine to take full effect. If it doesn't work then, get back to me and we'll talk about referral. Liver damage is extremely rare - don't worry, but of course stop taking it and contact me if you experience nausea, stomach pain or other new symptoms."

I really don't believe in the sections approach for real clinical use. Most doctors will default to writing as shown above and not using the tool.

Which patient groups do you think would benefit most from this kind of structured response?
I think most patients would benefit from such a well structured and emphatic response. Feeling heard and acknowledged is very important for pretty much all patients.
Are there any immediate clinical or ethical concerns? we should be aware of before developing this further?
The most important (and hard) thing in this task, I think, is to make sure the LLM doesn't change the meaning of the answer, but still makes it sound more empathic. If the LLM subtly changes the doctor's message, medical and legal issues could arise. For example, the doctor perhaps is a bit reassuring but also asking the patient to pay attention and get back if he/she gets worse. But then the LLM might be too reassuring and the patient doesn't get back in time when the symptoms are worse. Of course subtle changes in meaning could happen in any number of ways and the LLM could be both too reassuring and too worried in tone.
Are there any immediate regulation concerns? Would this work with clinical standards regarding AI and sharing patient information?
For this to be run in a true clinical setting, many conditions need to be fulfilled. I'm not an expert, but here are some thoughts:
Medical device or not? (If it does not affect the clinical course in any way it may not be a medical advice, but this would have to be proven). The classification of this tool under regulations like the EU AI Act or MDR (Medical Device Regulation) is a critical hurdle. If the "empathizer" only modifies tone and does not alter clinical instructions, it might fall outside the scope of a medical device. However, the line is thin, if the AI inadvertently softens a warning or alters the perceived urgency of a symptom, it becomes a clinical decision-support tool, which carries much higher regulatory scrutiny and much stricter validation requirements.
Local LLM at the clinic or hosted service? It may be easier to implement if it can be run with local models. If the healthcare provider can self-host, they have full control over data. Also, this would allow for a "frozen" model which we know doesn't get updated and suddenly may produce different responses (perhaps starts adding its own medical advice).
Integrated in EHR or external chat software vs a separate piece of software? As I mentioned with the forms, if this is a separate "app" where we have to copy and paste text, no one will use it. It needs to live directly inside the EHR/messaging software (wherever the messages are written in the first place), so it’s just a single click or a shortcut within the existing workflow.
Review and approve changes: The clinician should ideally be able to easily see what the LLM changed and of course be able to edit it or "undo" the change completely.
Auditing possibilities - before and after change? There needs to be a way to see exactly what the doctor originally wrote versus what the AI wrote and what was finally sent. If a patient has a bad outcome and we need to look back at the logs, it must be possible to prove whether the error was in the original message or if the AI changed the meaning of the doctor's instructions.
